Glossary
Ablation: All processes that reduce the mass of a glacier.

Accumulation: All processes that add to the mass of a glacier.

Crevasse: A crack formed in glacier ice when tensile stresses exceed the tensile strength of the ice.

Debris Flow: A moving mass of rock fragments, soil, and mud, more than half of the particles being
larger than sand size.

Firn: Snow that has survived at least one ablation season but has not been transformed into glacier
ice.

Glacier: A perennial mass of ice, and possibly firn and snow, originating on the land surface by the
recrystallization of snow or other forms of solid precipitation and showing evidence of past or
present flow.

LiDAR (Light Detection And Ranging): A remote sensing method that uses light in the form of a
pulsed laser to measure ranges (variable distances) to the Earth. These light pulses: combined with
other data recorded by the airborne system: generate precise, three-dimensional information about
the shape of the Earth and its surface characteristics.

Multispectral: Operating in or involving several regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. In remote
sensing, a multispectral image can be thought of as an archive that has several subset images within
it, each appearing at different frequencies in the electromagnetic spectrum.

Orthomosaic: A photogrammetrically orthorectified image product mosaiced from an image
collection where the geometric distortion has been corrected and the imagery has been color balanced
to produce a seamless mosaic dataset.

Outburst Flood: A high-magnitude, low-frequency catastrophic flood involving the sudden release
of a large quantity of water. These can occur from a proglacial lake, failure of a glacier dam, rapid
melting of ice sheets, collapse of glacial moraines, and/or surge of water from within a glacier.

Panchromatic: Sensitive to all visible colors of the spectrum.

Perennial: Persisting for an indefinite time longer than one year.

Snowfield: A more or less extensive and persistent mass of snow.

Structure from Motion (SfM): A photogrammetric imaging technique for estimating three-
dimensional structures from two-dimensional image sequences coupled with location motion signals.

Glossary terms and definitions are from Bates and Jackson (1984) and Cogley and others (2011).

Executive Summary
Surface area of glaciers and perennial snow within Mount Rainier National Park were delineated
based on 2021 aerial Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and satellite imagery to document changes to
glaciers over the last 125 years. These extents were compared with previously completed databases
from 1896, 1913, 1971, 1994, 2009, and 2015. In addition to the glacial features mapped at the Park,
any snow patches noted in satellite- and fixed-wing- acquired aerial images in September 2021 were
mapped as perennial snowfields.

In 2021, Mount Rainier National Park contained a total of 28 named glaciers which covered a total of
75.496 ± 4.109 km2 (29.149 ± 1.587 mi2). Perennial snowfields added another 1.938 ± 0.112 km2
(0.748 ± 0.043 mi2), bringing the total perennial snow and glacier cover within the Park in 2021 to
77.434 ± 4.221 km2 (29.897 ± 1.630 mi2). The largest glacier at Mount Rainier was the Emmons
Glacier, which encompasses 10.959 ± 0.575 km2 (4.231 ± 0.222 mi2).

The change in glacial area from 1896 to 2021 was -53.812 km2 (-20.777 mi2), a total reduction of
41.6%. This corresponds to an average rate of -0.430 km2 per year (-0.166 mi2 × yr-1) during the
125-year period. Recent changes (between the 6-year period of 2015 to 2021) showed a reduction
of -3.262 km2 (-1.260 mi2) of glacial area, or a 4.14% reduction at a rate of -0.544 km2 per year
(-0.210 mi2 × yr-1). This rate is 2.23 times that estimated in 2015 (2009-2015) of -0.244 km2 per year
(-0.094 mi2 × yr-1).

Changes in ice volume at Mount Rainier and estimates of total volumes were calculated for 1896,
1913, 1971, 1994, 2009, 2015, and 2021. Volume change between 1971 and 2007/8 was -0.65 km3
(-0.16 mi3; Sisson et al., 2011). We used the 2007/8 LiDAR digital elevation model and our 2021
SfM digital surface model to estimate a further loss of -0.404 km3 (-0.097 mi3). In the 50-year period
between 1971 and 2021, the glaciers and perennial snowfields of Mount Rainier lost a total of -1.058
km3 (-0.254 mi3) at a rate of -0.021 km3 per year (-0.005 mi3 × yr-1).

The calculation of the total volume of the glaciers during various glacier extent inventories at Mount
Rainier is not straightforward and various methods are explored in this paper. Using back calculated
scaling parameters derived from a single volume measurement in 1971 and estimates completed by
other authors, we have developed an estimate of glacial mass during the last 125-years at Mount
Rainier that mostly agree with volumetric changes observed in the last 50 years. Because of the high
uncertainty with these methods, a relatively modest 35% error is chosen.

In 2021, Mount Rainier’s 28 glaciers contain about 3.516 ± 1.231 km3 (0.844 ± 0.295 mi3) of glacial
ice, snow, and firn. The change in glacial mass over the 125-year period from 1896 to 2021
was -3.742 km3 (-0.898 mi3), a total reduction of 51.6%, at an average rate of -0.030 km3 per year
(-0.007 mi3 × yr-1). Volume change over the 6-year period of 2015 to 2021 was -0.175 km3 (-0.042
mi3), or a 4.75% reduction, at a rate of -0.029 km3 per year (-0.007 mi3 × yr-1).




                                                  xv
This survey officially removes one glacier from the Park’s inventory and highlights several other
glaciers in a critical state. The Stevens Glacier, an offshoot of the Paradise Glacier on the Park’s
south face, was removed due to its lack of features indicating flow, and therefore is no longer a
glacier but instead a perennial snowfield. Two other south facing glaciers – the Pyramid and Van
Trump glaciers – are in serious peril. In the six-year period between 2015 and 2021, these two
glaciers lost 32.9% and 33.6% of their area and 42.0% and 42.9% of their volume, respectively.
These glaciers are also becoming exceedingly fragmented and no longer possess what can be called a
main body of ice. Continued losses will quickly lead to the demise of these glaciers in the coming
decades. Overall, the glaciers on the south face of the mountain have been rapidly shrinking over the
last 125 years.

Our data shows a continuation of gradual yet accelerating loss of glacial ice at Mount Rainier,
resulting in significant changes in regional ice volume over the last century. The long-term impacts
of this loss will be widespread and impact many facets of the Park ecosystem. Additionally, rapidly
retreating south-facing glaciers are exposing large areas of loose sediment that can be mobilized to
proglacial rivers during rainstorms, outburst floods, and debris flows.

Regional climate change is affecting all glaciers at Mount Rainier, but especially those smaller cirque
glaciers and discontinuous glaciers on the south side of the volcano. If the regional climate trend
continues, further loss in glacial area and volume parkwide is anticipated, as well as the complete
loss of small glaciers at lower elevations with surface areas less than 0.2 km2 (0.08 mi2) in the next
few decades.

1. Introduction
Glaciers are an important geologic, geomorphic, biologic, and aquatic resource at Mount Rainier
National Park (MORA) and other associated landscapes around the world. The presence of glaciers
on Mount Rainier not only shapes the edifice (Lescinsky and Sisson, 1998) but provides a year-round
source of water for aquatic ecosystems in the Park. Glaciers also provide insight into the climate of
temperate alpine regions and studying the change in glaciers over time can illustrate the impacts of
climate change in the region (Meier, 1998; US EPA, 2016) and downstream impacts like freshwater
resources to aquatic organisms (Frans et al., 2018). Glacier surfaces can respond immediately to
accumulation and ablation of snow and ice, but it may take decades to centuries for the glacier’s
extents to respond (Bahr et al., 1998; Cuffey and Paterson, 2006; Hooke, 2020).

Glaciers at Mount Rainier also have been the source for damaging outburst floods during the Park’s
history. The Nisqually, Kautz, South Tahoma, Van Trump, and Winthrop glaciers at MORA have all
unleashed outburst floods since the 1950s (Copeland, 2009; Stevens et al., 2016; Wokosin et al.,
2017; Beason et al., 2021). These floods are not caused by failure of ice-dammed lakes or geothermal
heating, but by warm temperatures or rainfall events (Hoblitt et al., 1998; Neiman et al., 2008;
Neiman et al., 2011), and during periods extended warm surface air temperatures (Beason et al.,
2021). These glacial outburst floods can quickly mobilize into debris flows due to an overabundance
of loose, unstable sediment that compose steep glacial moraines. Such outburst floods and debris
flows have impacted Park infrastructure and have led to changes in Park management in large
geographic areas at MORA (Walder and Driedger, 1994; Beason et al., 2021).

Monitoring the changing mass of glaciers is necessary to address how climate change will affect Park
resources in the future. At present, glacial monitoring at MORA encompasses the following study
efforts: (1) mass balance; (2) surface elevations; (3) volume; (4) area; and (5) other miscellaneous
studies.

Mass balance studies have been conducted on the Nisqually and Emmons glaciers at MORA since
2003 as part of the North Coast and Cascades Inventory and Monitoring Network (NCCN I&M) as
one of a dozen major vital signs for region Parks (Riedel et al., 2010; National Park Service, 2012;
Hoffman et al., 2014; Riedel and Larrabee, 2015). Surface elevation studies on the Nisqually Glacier
have been conducted yearly since the 1930s but are limited to three or four transects across the width
of the glacier (Heliker et al., 1983). Volume estimates, using surface geophysical methods, are
limited due to the difficulty of traversing the glacier safely (Driedger and Kennard, 1986). Surface
area measurements are relatively straightforward and easy to obtain for a large area using aerial
imagery (Skloven-Gill and Fountain, 2015). Other studies have been completed over the last decade
to address various mechanics of glaciers, including identifying surface velocities on glaciers at the
Park to delineate active and stagnant ice (Hodge, 1974; Heliker, et al., 1983; Walkup et al., 2019;
Todd et al., 2021), mapping supraglacial debris for a variety of purposes (Dits and Moore, 2013;
Moore et al., 2014; Basehart et al., 2017; Moore et al., 2019; Fontana et al., 2021; Ireson et al.,
2021), methods for remotely monitoring glaciers (Medley, 2008; Shean, 2017; Whorton et al., 2017a;


                                                  1
Todd et al., 2022) and time-lapse photography to document glacial motion (Beason and Kenyon,
2018).

This study documents changes in surface area for glaciers and perennial snowfields at MORA. This
study builds on extents of glaciers acquired in 1896, 1913, 1971, 1994, 2009, and 2015 (Russell,
1897; Matthes, 1914; Nylen, 2001; Robinson et al., 2010; Beason, 2017; George and Beason, 2017).
Like the 2015 season, the 2021 season proved to be an ideal year to study the changes in glacial
extent due to a low snow year and presence of clear, high-resolution (less than 0.4 m [1.3 ft] per
pixel) satellite- and airborne-acquired aerial imagery which allowed the delineation of glaciers and
perennial snowfields.

This work compliments other recent studies on glacial loss in the Cascade Range, including
volcanoes like Glacier Peak (Fordham, 2022), Mount Adams (Sitts et al., 2010), Mount Baker (Pelto,
2015), Mount Hood (Ellinger, 2010) and glaciers within Olympic National Park (Riedel et al., 2015;
Fountain et al., 2022) and North Cascades National Park (Granshaw and Fountain, 2006; Pelto, 2008;
Dick, 2013; Pelto, 2015; Riedel and Larrabee, 2016; Headman et al., 2017; Pelto, 2017; Whorton et
al., 2017b). Nearly all glaciers in the Cascade Range are retreating and thinning, with a notable
exception of the Crater Glacier at Mount St. Helens (Walder et al., 2010). For example, between
1904 and 2006, the area of glacial ice on Mount Adams has decreased by 49% (from 31.5 km2 [12.2
mi2] to 16.2 km2 [6.3 mi2]) (Sitts et al., 2010). In the Olympic Mountains in 2015, 184 alpine glaciers
had a combined area of 30.2 ± 0.95 km2 (11.7 ± 0.37 mi2) but have had a 34% decrease in combined
area since 1980 (Riedel et al., 2015) and losing half of their glacial area since 1900 (Fountain et al.,
2022). The volume loss for the glaciers in the Olympic Mountains has been estimated at 17-24% in
the 26-year period between 1987 and 2010.

In the North Cascades, combined glacier area in the Skagit River basin has decreased by 32.02 ± 1.60
km2 (12.36 ± 0.62 mi2), or a 19% decrease from 1959-2016 (Riedel and Larrabee, 2016). Glaciers in
the North Cascades have seen some of the most drastic retreats in the entire region. Between 1900
and 2009, the glacial area in the North Cascades decreased from 533.89 ± 22.77 km2 (206.14 ± 8.79
mi2) to 236.20 ± 12.60 km2 (91.20 ± 4.86 mi2), or a total area loss of approximately 56% (Dick,
2013). Dick (2013) notes that century-scale loss is associated with increasing regional temperatures
in winter and summer and that the precipitation record shows no statistically significant trend. Loss
of glacial area and associated volume loss of glacial ice represents a significant loss of freshwater
resources in the region, which can have compounding ecologic effects (Riedel et al., 2015; Riedel
and Larrabee, 2016).

2. Study Area
Mount Rainier is 4,392 m (14,410 ft) stratovolcano located approximately 70 km (45 mi) southeast of
Tacoma, Washington (Figure 1). Mount Rainier is the tallest mountain in Washington State and has
more glaciers and perennial ice than all other Cascade Volcanoes combined (Driedger and Kennard,
1986) and more than about 10% of the total ice area in the lower 48 states (Meier, 1998; Fountain et
al., 2017). Given its proximity to the Pacific Ocean, prevailing westerly winds, and significant
topographic prominence, Mount Rainier is a substantial orographic barrier that increases
precipitation locally, compared to adjacent lowland areas. At higher elevations, more snow
accumulates than melts, which creates the glaciers.




Figure 1. Mount Rainier National Park extent and study area.


Glaciers at Mount Rainier vary considerably in size, elevation range, and aspect. Those glaciers on
the north side of Mount Rainier are generally larger and have more volume (Driedger and Kennard,
1986). Glaciers are present at elevations between 1,100 m (3,600 ft) and 4,392 m (14,410 ft).
Glaciers and perennial snowfields account for approximately 8.5% of the total area of MORA.

Mount Rainier has been episodically active in the last 500,000 years, including at least 10 to 12
eruptions in the last 2,600 years (Sisson and Vallance, 2009). The mass of glaciers on Mount Rainier
highlights one of its largest geological hazards – lahars (Scott et al., 1995). Lahars (volcanic debris
flows) have occurred frequently at Mount Rainier over the past several thousand years and can recur
without warning, possibly triggered by earthquakes or by changes in the volcano’s hydrothermal

                                                   3
system (Scott et al., 1995). Eruptions at Mount Rainier have initiated large lahars that have inundated
the Puget Lowland as far as 100 km (62 mi) from the volcano (Crandell, 1971). Because of its far-
reaching lahar hazards, MORA has a “very high” threat and ranks as the third most hazardous
volcano in the nation (Ewert et al., 2018). In addition to these far-reaching lahars, local-scale debris
flows induced by hydrologic and surficial geomorphic processes represent a significant and frequent
management concern within MORA boundaries (Driedger and Fountain, 1989; Driedger and Walder,
1991; Walder and Driedger, 1994; Scott and Vallance, 1995; Vallance et al., 2003).

3. Previous Research
Glacier extents have been mapped during the last 125 years at Mount Rainier. While individual
glaciers were mapped prior to the 1890s (King, 1871), the first dataset available for the entire Park
was completed in 1896 (Russell, 1897; Figure 2). Subsequent parkwide glacier mapping occurred in
1913, 1971, and 1994 (Matthes, 1914; Nylen, 2001). A digitized map of Park glaciers and perennial
snowfields was completed following a LiDAR survey in 2007/2008 and was probably the highest
resolution map available to that point (Robinson et al., 2010).

The latest glacial extent update was based on the 2015 glacial extent as observed via satellite and

                                                    5
The older maps in 1896 and 1913 had poor spatial accuracy indicated by the offset of some
topographic features observed on modern maps. For example, the 1896 inventory show glaciers
which cross over significant topographic breaks (e.g., the lower Puyallup Glacier) and boundaries
between glaciers that do not match with modern mapping.

Glacial volume has only been directly measured one time in the Park’s history by Driedger and
Kennard (1986), using glacial extents from 1971 and surveys of ice thickness from ice penetrating
radar, corrected for slope, and used to develop subglacial contour maps from which glacial volumes
were calculated. This approach was used not only at MORA, but also at Mount Hood, Three Sisters,
and Mount Shasta volcanoes.

Other authors have estimated glacial volume using a volume-area scaling approach (Nylen, 2001).
Much research has been conducted with respect to this technique (Bahr et al., 1997; Bahr et al., 2015;
Radić et al., 2017). Bahr and others (2015) note that “over 60 papers since 1988 have been published
in the glaciological and environmental change literature containing applications of volume-area
scaling” for a variety of purposes. Bahr and others (2015) note some major misconceptions and
inappropriate applications for this type of analysis.

In contrast, volume change can be estimated more precisely by differencing digital elevation models.
This approach was first completed at MORA by Robinson and others (2010) and Sisson and others
(2011). These methods are interesting because they provide a high-confidence numeric change in
glacial volume between time periods which can help determine the accuracy of volume-area scaling
approaches used later in this paper.

4. Methods
4.1. Glacier Extent Delineation and Comparison with Previous Studies
Mapping of 2021 extents was completed by hand digitizing the glacier and perennial snow perimeter
from aerial imagery at a 1:1,000 scale in a similar manner to the 2015 glacier extent and debris cover
layers (Beason, 2017). In situations where perimeters were not clearly observable, results were
compared with previous inventories. Observable features that assisted with the mapping in aerial
photos included surface streams that originated from beneath glacial ice, wet supraglacial debris, and
crevasses. Glaciers that shared a common boundary (e.g., the Emmons and Winthrop glaciers)
maintained the position of the boundary from previous surveys in situ order to minimize area changes
because of shifting borders. Field verification was undertaken where possible. Due to the large aerial
expanse of this inventory, field verification targeted areas of safe access and ambiguity and was not
completed for all glaciers.

Aerial images were acquired over the entire Park from a fixed wing parkwide Structure from Motion
(SfM) flight conducted by Owyhee Air Research that occurred between September 21 to 24, 2021.
The images were high resolution (0.253-0.337 m [0.831-1.1.06 ft]) and panchromatic with a source
resolution of 1:961 to 1:1,274. A photographic orthomosaic of the entire Park was pinned to the
surface with ground control points. A digital surface model (DSM) was constructed from the
panchromatic imagery using Structure from Motion (SfM) methods (Shean et al., 2016). A DSM
differs from products like bare-earth LiDAR digital elevation models (DEM) in that the DSM
product cannot “see through” vegetation. This product would be similarly called a “highest hit”
LiDAR image since it only can see unvegetated features or vegetation and ground use. The lack of a
bare earth product here is not a concern in the alpine zone due to little or no canopy and vegetative
cover.

Two separate orthomosaics were developed from the 21-24 September 2021 SfM flights (Table 1).
The “MORAsfm_WestOrthomosaic_20210921” file covers the western bound and high-
mountain areas of MORA. This set is the primary imagery used to map glaciers and snow in the Park
due to its more comprehensive coverage of the alpine zone but does not fully cover the eastern
flanks. The National Standard for Spatial Data Accuracy (NSSDA) horizontal accuracy of the
orthomosaic is 0.337 m (1.106 ft) with a source resolution of 1:1,274 and was assembled from
images acquired from the 21 September 2021 flight. The
“MORAsfm_EastOrthomosaic_2021092324” file covers the northern, southern, and eastern
bounds of MORA with partial coverage of the eastern flank of the volcano. This set has the overlap
needed to complete the eastern sections not observed during the western collections day (21
September 2021) such as the lower Emmons, Winthrop, Fryingpan, Ohanapecosh, Sarvent, and
Whitman glaciers. The NSSDA horizontal accuracy of the orthomosaic is 0.253 m (0.830 ft) with a
source resolution of 1:961 and was assembled from images acquired from the 23-24 September 2021
flights.

4.2. Glacier Statistics
The DSM datasets described in Section 4.1 were used to calculate topographic characteristics of
individual glaciers delineated in 2021. The topographic data was clipped to each delineated glacier to
determine maximum and minimum elevations for each glacier using the Zonal Statistics tool in
ArcGIS. Total relief is the range between minimum and maximum elevations for each glacier.
Glacier length is the distance along a line from the most upstream to most downstream point on each
glacier, while broadly following curves or bends in the glacier. Average slope (degrees) was
calculated using the Slope tool in ArcGIS, summarized per glacier with the Zonal Statistics tool
(ESRI, 2016).

4.3. Uncertainty of 2021 inventory
Digitizing glacial boundaries from remote imagery introduces a number of errors, including: (1)
georeferencing uncertainty of the image; (2) accuracy of digitizing the perimeter; and (3)
interpretation uncertainty. DeVisser and Fountain (2015) regard the later as the most significant
because of poor image quality, debris cover, and snow.

Imagery errors depend on the source of the image. For example, some satellite images have a
horizontal position error (CE90 – Circular Error, 90% percentile) of approximately 5 m (16 ft)
(DigitalGlobe, 2016), whereas NAIP images impose a standard that 95% of well-defined points
tested fall within 6 m (20 ft) of true ground points (Eckert, 2011). Horizontal accuracy of vertices
placed along glacier margins are a function of the scale of the map created and can be quantified by
using standards employed by the United States Geological Survey’s National Mapping Program
standards (USGS, 1999). Such standards state the following: “horizontal accuracy as 90% of all
measurable points must be within 1/30th of an inch for maps at a scale of 1:20,000 or larger.” For the
purposes of this study, the horizontal accuracy of a point at 1:1,000 scale is 0.847 m (2.778 ft) based
on the USGS nomenclature.

4.4. Volumetric Change of Glaciers – Measurements from DEMs and DSMs
Estimates of total change in glacial volume is a relatively easy measurement when provided with
digital elevation models and using tools in geographic information software. Sisson and others
(2011) used a National Elevation Dataset digital elevation model from 1:24,000 topographic maps
produced by photogrammetry of September 1970. The digital elevation map was then compared to
the 2007/8 LiDAR dataset acquired for the Park (Watershed Sciences, 2009). Differencing the DEMs
yields a total loss of elevation change at individual points on the glacier, which can then be summed
to calculate a volume loss per glacier.

A similar approach was used to summarize the volume loss between 2007/8 LiDAR and the 2021
SfM DSM. The differencing in this step was clipped to the 2009 glacier extent to summarize the
changes in glacial and proglacial areas between 2009 and 2021 (since there was no advance in
glaciers in this period; otherwise, the 1971 extent would be utilized).

scaling parameter, c, can provide an approximation of simplified glacial volume, V, from mapped
areas, A (Chen and Ohmura, 1990; Bahr et al., 2015; Radić et al., 2017) in Equation 5:

Bahr and others (2015) argue, on theoretical grounds, that the scaling exponent, γ, is a constant of
1.375. The scaling parameter, c, can vary as necessary to account for climatic region, topographic

5.2. Glaciers Removed or Listed as Endangered in the 2021 Inventory
Glaciers are a “perennial mass of ice, and possibly firn and snow, originating on the land surface by the
recrystallization of snow or other forms of solid precipitation and showing evidence of past or present
flow” (Cogley et al., 2011). The definition does not specify a minimum size for a perennial snowfield to
be named as a glacier. Fountain and others (2017), in their inventory of glaciers in the western US,
suggest that around 0.01 km2 (0.004 mi2) the error of omission is roughly equal to commission.
Further 0.01 km2 (0.004 mi2) seems to be the evolving international threshold for counting glaciers
(Fountain et al., 2017).

While all glaciers at Mount Rainier are retreating, and – as the total volumetric change and mass
change from 1896 to 2021 show – thinning, the glaciers on the south side of MORA are disappearing
faster than glaciers on the north. Several of these glaciers are rapidly retreating, thinning, and
fragmenting into multiple, smaller, individual pieces.

The conditions that have led to the formation of the Stevens Glacier include many factors that have
placed it in a vulnerable state. It is a south-facing glacier that is an offshoot of the Paradise Glacier;
the Paradise Glacier was already a feature that has had a rapid loss of glacial and ice due to the lower
Paradise Glacier becoming cut off from its accumulation zone by bedrock topography. The famous
former Paradise ice caves (which disappeared in the 1980s [NPS, 1989]) and the nearby Williwakas
Glacier (which was noted in 1931 to be a “dead glacier” [NPS, 1931]) were the first cryospheric
features that disappeared in the 20th century and a harbinger of things to come for glaciers in this
area.

The Stevens Glacier in 2021 (Figure 4) showed no obvious evidence of movement (e.g., crevasses),
had no connection to any larger glacial mass above it (providing future glacial ice accumulation to
the system), and had an area of about 0.033 km2 (8 acres) in 2015 and 0.029 km2 (7 acres) in 2021.
The feature is essentially a perennial snowfield. Therefore, we remove the Stevens Glacier from the
Park’s glacier inventory and reduce the total number of glaciers at Mount Rainier to 28.

Several other south-facing glaciers have lost massive amounts of area and mass in 2021 relative to
2015. While these glaciers are still showing some signs of movement, several have fragmented
significantly. The two most at risk for future ice loss are the Pyramid and Van Trump glaciers. In the
six-year period between 2015 and 2021, these two glaciers lost 32.9% and 33.6% of their area and
42.0% and 42.9% of their volume, respectively. These glaciers are “critically endangered” and
should be carefully watched over the next decade to determine whether they are still meeting the
definition of a glacier. On the north side of the Park and to a lesser extent, the Flett, Inter, and
Sarvent glaciers are also at elevated risk for future loss. These three glaciers should be considered
“endangered” and monitored over the next several decades.

From a hazard perspective, it should be noted that glaciers bind up loose and unconsolidated
sediment on steep slopes that mantle the volcano. Both the Pyramid and Van Trump glaciers have
been sources of debris flows in the November 2006 atmospheric river storm that dropped about 46
cm (18 in) of rain in 36 hours (Copeland, 2009). In prior decades, they have been sources of debris
flows several times over the last two decades (Walder and Driedger, 1994). Without glacier ice, the
unstable landscape surrounding former glaciers in the Park is prone to significant erosion and bank
collapse, potentially creating increased debris flow activity. Mechanisms like these have been noted
to initiate debris flows at Mount Rainier (Lancaster et al., 2012) and other Cascade Volcanoes (De La
Fuente et al., 2015; Mikulovsky et al., 2015). These potential source locations at Mount Rainier are
all upslope of significant tracts of Park infrastructure and introduce increasing risk to some of these
locations.

5.5. Volumetric Change from 1970 to 2021
Volumetric change * was calculated by Sisson and others (2011) and Robinson and others (2010) to
estimate total change in glacier volume from a period of early 1970 to 2007/8 (Figure 11). The
authors determined that the differencing between the two time periods gives a net volumetric change
for glaciers and perennial snowfields of -0.65 km3 (-0.16 mi3). They also note that because the 1970
DEM may be up to 0.7 m (2.3 ft) high relative to the LiDAR, the minimum net volumetric change is
-0.59 km3 (-0.14 mi3). Finally, Sisson and others (2011) note that Mount Rainier’s glaciers and
perennial snowfields lost approximately 13.4 – 14.7% of their 1970-1981 composite volume.

Figure 12 and Table 11 show the results of the differencing between 2007/8 and 2021, using a scale
that matches the pattern used in Figure 1 by Sisson and others (2011). Glaciers and perennial
snowfields during this time lost, on average, between -0.54 to -15.71 m (-1.77 to -51.54 ft), with an
average of -4.63 m (-15.19 ft). The total volumetric change between 2007/8 and 2021 is -0.404 km3
(-0.097 mi3) (Table 11). The average volumetric change for each glacier in the Park was -0.014 km3
(-0.003 mi3).

Combining the two periods gives a total net volumetric change at the Park between 1970 to 2021 of
between -0.994 to -1.054 km3 (-0.239 to -0.253 mi3).




*
 For the purposes of this study, the term “volumetric change” refers to the total change in volume between time
periods at MORA, whereas “volume” refers to the total volume present at a specific point in time. For example,
glacier volumes at Time A and B of 0.9 and 0.8 km3 respectively would result in a volumetric change of -0.1 km3
during that period.

As the mean, Table 15’s data will be used when discussing volumes for individual glaciers or
parkwide glacial totals for the 1896-2021 period from here forward. Data from Table 13 or 14 could
also be used in a similar way (bookending the estimates), but such figures have not been created for

The lower left inset in Figure 14 are the calculations of volumetric change between 1971-2009 and
2009-2021, which can be directly compared to volumetric change results in Section 5.5. The average
volumetric change between 1971-2009 is -0.634 km3 (-0.152 mi3), while the 2009-2021 volumetric
change is -0.374 km3 (-0.090 mi3), for a total of -1.008 km3 (-0.241 mi3). The results for each of
these periods mirrors the observations of volumetric change between 1971-2009 and 2009-2021,
giving some confidence in the approaches used to calculate volume at different time periods
presented here.

Overall, in the last 125 years, Mount Rainier has lost approximately 3.743 km3 (0.898 mi3), a rate of
about -0.030 km3 per year (-0.007 mi3 × yr-1) (Figure 14, Table 15). The trends observed in Figure 14
(volume) mirror those in Figure 4 (extent).

Figure 15 shows the changes in glacial volume for those glaciers and glacial systems (e.g., Nisqually
and Wilson Glaciers together) whose 2021 surface area is greater than (or near) 5 km2 (1.9 mi2). It
should be noted that the North Mowich Glacier has an extent less than 5 km2; it is included in Figure
15 to be consistent with a similar figure in Beason (2017). Figure 16 shows the volume of glaciers
and glacial systems whose area is between 1 – 5 km2 (0.4 – 1.9 mi2) and Figure 17 shows the volume
of glaciers and glacial systems whose area is less than 1 km2 (0.4 mi2).

Table 16 shows the change in estimated volume between inventory periods for each glacier at Mount
Rainier, based on mean volume change from Table 15. Table 17 shows the total percentage change in
estimated volume between inventory periods for each glacier at Mount Rainier, based on data mean
volume change from Table 15. These tables are useful as they show individual glaciers that are
handling changing climate better or worse than others in the Park (for example, south facing cirque
glaciers versus north facing summit glaciers).

The calculations in Table 15 result in volume changes over time that roughly agree with the
volumetric changes in glacier mass between 1971 to 2007/8 (Sisson et al., 2011) and 2007/8 to 2021
(this study). Table 15’s average volumetric change between 1971-2009 is -0.610 km3 (-0.146 mi3),
which is in close agreement to the -0.59 to -0.65 km3 (-0.14 to -0.16 mi3) mass loss calculated by
Sisson and others (2011). The 2009 to 2021 period has an average volumetric change in Table 15
of -0.374 km3 (-0.090 mi3), which compares with the volumetric change of -0.404 km3 (-0.097 mi3).
This second period has a lower confidence but is within the error margin of the volume calculation
method.

5.8. Recommendations for Future Work
Understanding the interactions between retreating glaciers and their associated ecosystems are one of
the key benefits of these kind of surveys. We recommend developing updated glacial extents layers
every five to 10 years, especially as more frequent digital elevation data becomes available (e.g.,
satellite and fixed-wing SfM surveys and updated LiDAR datasets). Digital elevation data is a
primary source of volumetric change in various timeframes and the only real source of high-
resolution and high-accuracy volume changes in the Park.

Total glacial volume in each survey period were estimated in this work and calibrated to a single
estimate of glacial mass from glacial extents in 1971 and measurements of subglacial topography
measured in the early 1980s Driedger and Kennard (1986). Given that four to five decades have
passed since the work by Driedger and Kennard (1986), a new comprehensive look at the glacial
volume of Mount Rainier and other Cascade Volcanoes is sorely needed. Despite having a good idea
of volumetric changes in glacial ice between 1971-2007/8 and 2007/8-2021, we still lack low-error
values for glacial volume at specific times during the last 125 years. Notwithstanding the caveats for
volume estimates, the data presented here are still quite useful as a method of understanding not only
how glacial ice is decreasing in area, but also how it is decreasing from the “top down”. This area of
study would benefit a wide variety of work in aquatic, geomorphic, and geohazard recognition for a
wide-ranging audience.

6. Conclusions
Glaciers are an important and complex cryospheric feature whose health is balanced between winter
accumulation and summer melt. They serve as a significant source of ice at Mount Rainier which
provides cool water in summer months to a variety of ecosystems. They also serve as the architects
of Mount Rainier, sculpting the lava flows during periods of volcanic unrest and continuously
wearing down the volcano during periods of volcanic quiescence. Glaciers are one of the best
indicators of climate change in a region since their surface features, extent, and volume all respond to
the long-term balances of accumulation and ablation of snow.

With the exception of some periods in the 1960s to 1980s, glaciers at MORA have been in an overall
retreat since the late 1800s. The implications of this retreat are widespread, affecting everything from
aquatic organisms to the fluvial geomorphology of proglacial braided rivers. Damaging outburst
floods unleashed by glaciers and the voluminous sediment left behind by retreating glaciers have led
to scores of debris flows at MORA, which has led to changes in Park management in large swaths of
land throughout MORA.

Studying the long-term trends at Mount Rainier glaciers reveals important insights into the health of
Park glaciers and provides key data to regional glacier studies. These studies complement each other
and provide a look at the glacial loss in the Cascade Range. Loss of glacial area and associated
volume loss of glacial ice throughout the Cascades represents a significant loss of freshwater
resources in the region, which can have compounding ecologic and geomorphic effects.

Technology like SfM has allowed a tremendous new opportunity to capture extremely large amounts
of data quickly and inexpensively. This photographic and digital elevation data serve as the basis for
the 2021 glacial update at MORA and will allow for future high-resolution surveys to monitor the
glacial resources at the Park.

All 28 glaciers at Mount Rainier are in retreat, losing an average of -0.430 km2 per year (-0.166 mi2 ×
yr-1) parkwide during the last 125 years. Since 2015, this rate has increased to -0.544 km2 per year
(-0.210 mi2 × yr-1) parkwide. When looking at volumetric data, the Park has lost a total of -1.057 km3
(-0.254 mi3) of glacial mass since 1970, at a rate of about -0.021 km3 per year (-0.005 mi2 × yr-1). As
of 2021, the Park contains 75.496 ± 4.109 km2 (29.149 ± 1.587 mi2) and about 3.516 ± 1.231 km3
(0.844 ± 0.295 mi3) of glacial ice.

This survey removes the Stevens Glacier from the Park’s glacier inventory. The Stevens Glacier has
been languishing for decades in a south-facing, low elevation location that is physically detached
from a higher accumulation zone. While it may still serve as a perennial snowfield, it no longer
contributes to the Park’s glacier inventory. Two other south-facing glaciers at Mount Rainier are in a
significantly perilous state. The Pyramid and Van Trump glaciers lost 32.9% and 33.6% of their area
and 42.0% and 42.9% of their volume in the last six years, respectively, and have become
exceedingly fragmented. These glaciers may only have a few decades left being listed on the Park’s
glacier inventory if climate trends do not change.


                                                  53
The glaciers at Mount Rainier are in a long-term demise. Our data shows a gradual yet accelerating
loss of glacial ice at Mount Rainier, resulting in significant changes in regional ice volume over the
last century. The long-term impacts of this loss will be widespread and impact many facets of the
Park ecosystem. Additionally, rapidly retreating south-facing glaciers are exposing vast sources of
loose sediment that can be provided to proglacial rivers during warm rainstorms, outburst floods, and
debris flows. Monitoring the glacial and proglacial resources at MORA over the next century will be
key to understanding the long-term impacts of loss of glacial ice to the management of the Park and
effects to the region.
